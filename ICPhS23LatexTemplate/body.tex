So far, I have examined the psychological validity of hidden Markov models (HMMs) in modeling phoneme perception, particularly focusing on replicating the phenomenon of illusory vowels through computational experiments. These models demonstrated the necessity of parallel processing in accurately capturing perceptual epenthesis related to phonotactic constraints and acoustic cues. Building on these findings, Chapter 3 transitions from computational modeling to a psycholinguistic study, investigating how allophones influence phoneme perception. Specifically, I will explore the impact of dialectal differences, such as vowel devoicing in the Tokyo and Kansai dialects, on the perception of illusory vowels. This chapter aims to evaluate how both phonotactic and allophonic variations contribute to perceptual epenthesis, using human behavioral data to complement the computational insights gained in the previous chapter.

\section{Introduction}

As discussed in previous chapters, when hearing speech sounds that violate the phonotactic rules of their native language, listeners tend to perceive the phonemes in accordance with those rules. For example, native Japanese speakers perceive [ebzo] as /ebuzo/ due to the phonotactic constraints of Japanese, inserting an \textit{illusory vowel} \parencite{dupoux1999epentheticvi, dupoux2011illusory}. Vowel devoicing has been shown to influence this illusion \parencite{kilpatrick2018japanese}. In this study, we re-examine these effects by conducting experiments on speakers of different Japanese dialects, each with varying degrees of vowel devoicing following specific consonants, and discuss how these findings should be incorporated into perceptual models.

Phonotactics play a key role in understanding the vowel illusion \parencite{halle2014special, monahan2009not, mattingley2015influence}, as demonstrated in an experiment comparing native French and Japanese speakers \parencite{dupoux1999epentheticvi}. In contrast to French, Japanese prohibits consonant clusters like /bz/, and the study tested the ability of speakers of both languages to discriminate between [ebuzo] and [ebzo]. While French speakers successfully distinguished the two sequences, Japanese speakers perceived [ebzo] as /ebuzo/, resulting in lower discrimination accuracy.

Furthermore, experiments with native speakers of Brazilian Portuguese and Japanese have highlighted the influence of formant cues on illusory vowel perception \parencite{dupoux2011illusory}. Neither language permits the /bz/ cluster, and the default epenthetic vowels were /i/ and /u/ for Brazilian Portuguese and Japanese, respectively. In this study, the vowels between /b/ and /z/ in [ebizo] and [ebuzo] were deleted, leaving only the acoustic cues of the vowels in /b/. These remnants of formant cues affected the participants of both languages, increasing the insertion rate of the non-default vowel /i/ among Japanese participants.

One-step models can account for these findings, as they posit that phonotactic rules and acoustic cues simultaneously influence vowel illusion \parencite{dupoux2011illusory}. These one-step models can be represented by hidden Markov models (HMMs), which also encompass perceptual assimilation models \parencite{best2001discrimination} and exemplar theory models \parencite{lacerda1995perceptual}. Statistical inference models \parencite{feldman2009influence, wilson2013bayesian} have replicated the illusion of vowels \parencite{kishiyama2021influence} by calculating:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hmmch3}
    \hat{c} = \argmax_{c} P(c | c_{t-1})P(S_{\text{new}}|c).
\end{equation}
Eq. (\ref{eq:hmmch3}) computes two likelihoods: $P(c|c_{t-1})$, which represents the probability of transitioning from the previous phoneme $c_{t-1}$ to the current phoneme $c$, and $P(S_{\text{new}}|c)$, which represents the likelihood of the input speech sound $S_{\text{new}}$ given phoneme $c$. The phoneme $c$ that maximizes these combined likelihoods is then selected as $\hat{c}$.

Traditionally, all elements of $c$ referred to phonemes \parencite{wilson2013bayesian}. However, recent research suggests that allophones should be treated as discrete units within perceptual models, reflecting their role in speech perception more accurately.

First, devoiced or deleted vowels in Japanese have been reported to increase perceptual epenthesis \parencite{kilpatrick2018japanese}. In the Tokyo dialect, the high vowel /u/ in /esupo/ is often deleted, resulting in [espo] when it follows fricatives or affricates \parencite{fujimoto2003devoice_eng, shaw2018lingual}. In other cases, the vowel in /epuso/ is devoiced, resulting in [ep\textsubring{\textturnm}so]. Studies have found that deletion or devoicing encourages perceptual assimilation, leading to an increased likelihood of perceptual epenthesis. Second, transition probabilities between allophones have been shown to influence vowel illusions \parencite{kilpatrick2020japanese}. For example, the allophone [\textctc], a realization of /s/ before /i/, is not typically followed by other vowels. Research found that [\textctc{}] frequently induces the perception of /i/ \parencite{kilpatrick2020japanese}, more so than other consonants like [g]. They argued that this means that allophones and their transition probabilities contribute to the vowel illusion.

The key question is whether phoneme perception models should treat allophones as categorical entities, rather than as continuous. Without assuming categorical allophones, one-step models can explain the results described above. The variation in auditory cues within deleted or devoiced vowels results in different $P(S_{\text{new}}|c)$. Furthermore, the stimulus [\textctc] is thought to carry acoustic cues of /i/ \parencite{kubozono1999japanese_eng}, which can also influence $P(S_{\text{new}}|c)$. As such, these models can explain the results without needing to treat allophones as categorical.

The confounding of allophones and acoustic cues allows for multiple interpretations of the experimental results. For example, in the case of [ep\textsubring{\textturnm}so] and [espo], both the allophonic status (devoiced/deleted) and the preceding speech sounds ([p] vs. [s]) differ. Similarly, when comparing [egpo] and [e\textctc{}po], the transition probabilities from [g] or [\textctc] to the next vowel are confounded with the acoustic cues of these consonants.

To address this issue, we leveraged the dialectal differences between the Kansai and Tokyo dialects. We compared the discrimination accuracy of VCCV and VCVCV sequences in Tokyo-dialect speakers with that of Kansai-dialect speakers, who are reported to devoice or delete vowels less frequently \parencite{byun2011_eng, byun2012_eng}, while keeping the test stimuli constant. According to the hypothesis that deletion and devoicing induce vowel illusions, Kansai-dialect speakers, being less exposed to these allophones, should experience fewer illusions and produce more accurate responses in AXB discrimination tasks. \footnote{
    While it is clear that phonotactics cause Kansai speakers to perceive illusory vowels,
    the hypothesis that deletion induces epenthesis supports this prediction.
    By contrast, if both groups consistently perceive illusory vowels regardless of their exposure to devoicing or deletion,
    then the expected effect of devoicing or deletion will not be observed in Tokyo-dialect speakers.
    This issue will be further discussed in Chapter 4.
}.

\section{Perception experiment}

\subsection{Materials and methods}

Participants were recruited through an outsourcing service, selecting individuals aged 18 or older from either the Tokyo or Kansai regions. Priority was given to participants who had lived exclusively in these regions since birth. A total of 62 participants were recruited, consisting of 33 Tokyo speakers (mean age: 38, $SD = 10.9$) and 29 Kinki speakers (mean age: 37, $SD = 11.9$). Data from four participants were excluded due to accuracy rates below 75\% in the categorization task (\S3). All procedures followed ethical guidelines approved by the institution, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

In the AXB task, participants were asked to distinguish between VC$_\text{1}$VC$_\text{2}$V and VC$_\text{1}$C$_\text{2}$V target stimuli. A total of 32 stimuli items were presented, based on a combination of the following four conditions: (1) For environments where vowel deletion and devoicing occur, we selected four consonant pairs: s--p, k--t, p--s, and ts--k for C$_\text{1}$C$_\text{2}$. (2) Stimuli included VC$_\text{1}$VC$_\text{2}$V with /u/ between C$_\text{1}$ and C$_\text{2}$, which participants were asked to discriminate from VC$_\text{1}$C$_\text{2}$V. (3) Trials varied depending on whether the X-position stimulus was VC$_\text{1}$VC$_\text{2}$V or VC$_\text{1}$C$_\text{2}$V. (4) Voiced counterparts of C$_\text{1}$ and C$_\text{2}$, such as b--z, were also included, where no deletion or devoicing occurs. Correct answer positions (A or B) were counterbalanced.

The stimuli were recorded by three male speakers, and their loudness was adjusted to 75 dB. The duration preceding C$_\text{2}$ was approximately 50 ms (approximately 400 ms for all three moras) to prevent the perception of the geminate consonant (\textit{sokuon}), a distinctive phoneme in Japanese. Voicing parts between consonants in /egudo/ and /ebuzo/ were removed based on formant and power, and the silent interval was extended by duplicating and extending the preceding waveform cycle. In contrast, the /u/ in /epuso/ was shortened to match the duration of /epuso/.

Following three practice trials, 32 target items and 24 filler items were randomized and presented through a web browser in a controlled experimental environment. A fixation cross (``+'') was displayed in the center of the screen for 1000 ms before the audio stimuli, and the AXB audio sequences were presented with 200 ms intervals between them. After the audio presentation, participants were instructed to respond as quickly as possible. No feedback on the correctness of their responses was given during the experimental trials to avoid a learning effect, with feedback only provided during the practice tasks. Participants initiated each subsequent trial by pressing the space key.

We examined the distribution of reaction times and excluded 0.61\% of data points where the reaction time exceeded 10 seconds as outliers. The mean accuracy was calculated by averaging across four conditions: X in AXB (either VC$_\text{1}$VC$_\text{2}$V or VC$_\text{1}$C$_\text{2}$V) and correct answer position (A or B). Missing values were excluded from the analysis.

We then constructed a Bayesian linear mixed-effects model \parencite{lme4, rstanarm, easystats}, using the Tokyo--Kansai ratio as the independent variable and AXB task discrimination accuracy as the dependent variable. Items and participants were included as random effects. The analysis revealed an overall mean accuracy of 0.59, with no significant effect of environmental differences. The estimate for dialectal differences was positive (0.019), but it did not indicate a decline in precision.

To further assess the null hypothesis, we calculated the Bayes factor ($BF$) for the Tokyo--Kansai ratio. The Bayes factor, used to compare models within the Bayesian framework, was 0.150, providing substantial evidence in favor of the null hypothesis. In this study, the $BF$ was calculated as $Posterior \text{ }Odds / Prior \text{ }Odds$, where the odds were determined as $P(b\notin[0, \infty] | Data)/P(b\in[0, \infty] | Data)$ and $P(b\notin[0, \infty])/P(b\in[0, \infty])$, respectively. Since the estimates of the Tokyo--Kansai ratio ($b$) were expected to be negative, the null region \parencite{kruschke2010believe}—which assumes no effect—was set as a positive value [0, $\infty$].

The $BF$ of 0.15 for the Tokyo--Kansai ratio suggests that the null hypothesis of a positive effect is $1/0.15 = 6.67$ times more plausible than the alternative hypothesis. This finding contradicts the assumption that allophonic variation increases the rate of illusions and reduces discrimination accuracy. \footnote{https://github.com/kishiyamat/icphs2023}.

If these allophones triggered perceptual epenthesis, Tokyo dialect speakers would be expected to have lower accuracy in the discrimination task compared to speakers who experience less devoicing. However, the results from the AXB task showed that the discrimination accuracy for both Kansai and Tokyo dialect speakers, regardless of their language background, remained around 0.59---close to chance level. Furthermore, the Bayes factor ($BF$) analysis indicated that neither language experience nor devoicing significantly impacted the rate of perceptual illusions in the discrimination task.

Additionally, we explored whether differences in dialect-based language experience influenced participants' offline goodness ratings for deletion and devoicing. It is important to note that this categorization task was conducted after the online process to prevent any potential interference with the validation of the online task results.

\section{Categorization task}

\subsection{Materials and methods}

Participants who completed the perceptual task also took part in a categorization task. In this task, all the sounds presented during the perceptual task were categorized and rated. There were eight different sound patterns: four that were devoiced in the Tokyo dialect (esupo, ekuto, epuso, etsuko) and four that were not devoiced (ezubo, egudo, ebuzo, edzugo). Each pattern had a devoiced counterpart, and the choices were displayed on the screen using Japanese characters. Since three speakers recorded the 16 patterns, participants listened to a total of 48 stimuli, selected the option that most closely matched each sound, and rated its acceptability on a 7-point scale (0--6). In the rating, zero indicated ``not appropriate at all,'' while six signified ``completely appropriate.''

\subsection{Results}

Figure \ref{fig:cat_results} displays the relationship between the Tokyo--Kansai residential ratio ($x$-axis) and the goodness of fit rating ($y$-axis). The left and right panes distinguish between the devoicing and no-devoicing environments, respectively. Color differences show whether the actual stimulus was devoiced or not. Overall, ratings were above the midpoint of 4. Devoiced stimuli were rated higher in the devoicing environment (left pane) and lower in the no-devoicing environment (right pane). The participants' residential history did not appear to significantly influence these patterns.

To statistically analyze these results, we constructed a Bayesian linear mixed-effects model, with the goodness of fit rating as the dependent variable. The independent variables included the environment (devoicing/no-devoicing), stimulus type (devoiced/not devoiced), the Tokyo--Kansai residential ratio, and their interactions. Random effects for participants and items were included. A normal distribution ($M=0$, $SD=10$) was used as a prior distribution for each parameter.

The mean rating was 4.87, indicating that participants generally found the stimuli to be natural, considering the rating scale ranged from 0 to 6. The estimates for the devoicing environment and devoiced stimuli were $-0.37$ and $-0.79$, respectively. Additionally, the interaction between the devoiced stimulus and the devoicing environment increased the rating by 1.19. The Bayes factor ($BF$) for the Tokyo--Kansai residential ratio was 0.012, with the null region set to $[-\infty, 0]$, providing very strong evidence for the null hypothesis.

\subsection{Discussion}

The results showed that the overall ratings exceeded 4, indicating that the quality of the stimuli used in this study was comparable to those in previous studies \parencite{kilpatrick2018japanese}. If participants could categorize devoiced speech in the Tokyo dialect without perceiving an epenthetic vowel, the ratings for devoiced stimuli in a no-devoicing environment would be expected to fall below 3. However, both Tokyo and Kansai dialect speakers exhibited higher ratings when the stimuli were devoiced in a devoicing environment, and lower ratings when devoiced stimuli were presented in a no-devoicing environment. Despite this pattern, the interaction effect ($b = 1.19$) was not substantial enough to shift the perception of the devoiced stimuli from unnatural to fully natural.

\section{General discussion}

Previous research has suggested that devoicing may contribute to perceptual epenthesis. Additionally, probabilistic models have accounted for the distribution of auditory realizations. Based on the results from both the perceptual experiments and the categorization tasks in this study, two alternative interpretations are proposed.

First, the observed effects in discrimination accuracy and categorization tasks from previous studies may not be directly attributed to devoicing, but rather to acoustic differences. If devoicing or deletion were the primary cause of perceptual epenthesis, we would expect Tokyo dialect speakers, who commonly devoice or delete vowels, to show lower discrimination accuracy compared to Kansai dialect speakers, who seldom exhibit these patterns. However, no significant difference was observed between the two groups in this study. Both Kansai and Tokyo dialect speakers perceived illusory vowels at similar rates. However, if both groups consistently perceive illusory vowels due to phonotactic constraints, the comparison becomes difficult because of a ceiling effect. In other words, if Kansai-dialect speakers already perceive illusory vowels nearly 100\% of the time, there is no additional margin for Tokyo-dialect speakers to perceive them at a higher rate.

Second, in terms of how acoustic cues influence speech perception, previous studies \parencite{feldman2009influence, wilson2013bayesian, kishiyama2021influence} have employed inference models based on Equation (\ref{eq:hmmch3}). In this framework, $P(S_{\text{new}}|c)$ represents the distribution of auditory realizations for phonemes or the perceptual likelihood of $S_{\text{new}}$ given phoneme $c$, without requiring a generative model. This is consistent with our findings, as $P$( [\textsubring{\textturnm}] | /u/ ) or $P$( [s] | /su/ ) is not zero, reflecting the exposure of Kansai dialect speakers to the Tokyo dialect, which is widely used across Japan. By applying Bayes' Theorem, the equation can be rewritten as:
\[
\hat{c} = \argmax_{c} \frac{P(c | c_{t-1}) P(S_{\text{new}}|c)}{P(c)},
\]
which integrates discriminative models rather than relying solely on generative models. This approach opens the possibility of using other discriminative techniques, such as neural networks or multinomial logistic regression, in speech perception modeling.

Given the previous validation of HMMs in explaining behavioral data \parencite{kishiyama2021influence, kishiyama2022onestep}, next chapter will explore how discriminative models can be incorporated into HMMs to further improve the explanation of human speech perception.
